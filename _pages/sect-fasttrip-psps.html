Understanding the key parameters

The optimization uses several inputs that you can think of as policy levers (budgets and caps), reliability safeguards, and statistical controls. Below is a short guide written for decision-makers and oversight staff.

Policy levers (budgets and caps)

Limits you set on how much of the system can use sectionalization, fast-trip, or affect customers.

Sectionalization (sect.) budget

What it is: The maximum number of circuits that can be sectionalized—i.e., prepared so they can be shut off in segments during a PSPS event instead of all at once.

Why it matters: A higher budget allows more granular shutoffs (fewer customers affected per event) but requires more upfront investment and coordination. Setting this is a tradeoff between operational flexibility and cost.

Controlled with: Sect. budget (ratio of circuits)

Fast-trip budget

What it is: The maximum number of circuits that can be assigned fast-trip settings—operational settings that reduce ignition risk by tripping more quickly under fault conditions.

Why it matters: More circuits with fast-trip can reduce wildfire risk but may increase momentary outages and affect reliability. The budget caps how much of the system uses this mitigation.

Controlled with: Fast-trip budget (ratio of circuits)

Reliability constraint

What it is: A cap on the total “reliability impact” allowed from both PSPS and fast-trip—typically expressed as a maximum amount of customer load (e.g., population or customer-hours) that can be affected by these interventions.

Why it matters: This directly ties to CPUC’s goals around reliability and customer exposure. A tighter constraint limits how much the utility can rely on shutoffs and fast-trip; a looser one allows more aggressive risk reduction at the cost of more customer impact.

Controlled with: Reliability constraint (ratio of pop.)

Reliability safeguards

Parameters that link mitigation choices to the reliability cap and protect customer exposure.

Fast-trip reliability impact

What it is: A scaling factor that describes how much each circuit’s fast-trip setting contributes to the overall reliability constraint (e.g., expected customer impact).

Why it matters: It links fast-trip decisions to the same reliability cap as PSPS. Higher values mean the model treats fast-trip as having a larger reliability cost, so it may use less fast-trip and more sectionalization or PSPS to stay within the cap.

Controlled with: Fast-trip average reliability impact

Statistical controls

How we group circuits and control false discoveries when identifying high-risk circuits.

Number of groups

What it is: The number of groups used when we statistically group circuits for controlling false discoveries (see FWER below). Circuits are clustered into this many groups before applying multiple-testing corrections.

Why it matters: More groups allow finer distinctions between high- and low-risk circuits but change how conservative the risk estimates are. Fewer groups are simpler and more conservative; more groups can improve targeting at the cost of more complexity.

Controlled with: Number of groups

Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER)

What it is: The target probability of making at least one false “high risk” call when testing many circuits at once. Often set at 0.05 (5%) or 0.10 (10%).

Why it matters: Lower FWER (e.g., 0.05) means we are more conservative—fewer circuits are labeled high risk, but we are less likely to miss a truly risky circuit. Higher FWER allows more circuits to be flagged as high risk, which can mean more mitigation but also more false positives. It is a key dial for balancing safety vs. over-mitigation.

Controlled with: FWER, Effectiveness of fast-trip

Declustering and multiple-testing method

What it is: The method used to (1) group nearby or similar ignition events (declustering) and (2) control false discoveries when testing many circuits (e.g., Bonferroni, MaxRank, or other MHT methods).

Why it matters: These choices affect which circuits are treated as high risk and thus which get mitigation. Different methods trade off conservatism, power, and interpretability—important for regulatory consistency and stakeholder communication.

Controlled with: Declustering method, Decluster + MHT method
